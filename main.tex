\abstract{Modellers of Malaria transmission often encounter structural modelling issues such as misspecification, where the model is not consistent with the true data generating process, and non-identifiability, where a parameter cannot be determined from observational data. Structural modelling issues are challenging to resolve given the vast space of admissible transmission models and non-unique resolutions. Navigating the landscape of admissible models requires a careful combination of prior knowledge and Bayesian model diagnostics. We propose an harness for proposing and evaluating likely malaria transmission models and an empirical analysis of the resulting landscape.}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Malaria transmission modelling}

% The complexity, immunity, drugs, vectors
Malaria transmission is a complex and challenging process to model. Part of its complexity comes from the multi-phase lifecycle of the Plasmodium parasite and its multiple interactions the human immune system and the non-linear effects of Force of Infection on malaria burden at different ages.

% The data: prevalence, incidence, EIR
Moreover, the available data is generally high variance and sparse, due to low study sizes and irregular collection intervals. Three measures of malaria burden are commonly used as observational data. From \citet{thesis}:

\begin{thesisquote}
\begin{description}
    \item[Clinical Malaria Incidence] This is the number of clinical malaria episodes within a specific time frame. An episode is typically defined as a new patient with a fever coupled with a positive diagnostic test. Incidence can be determined through active or passive case detection methods. Active case detection involves routinely monitoring a pre-enrolled cohort over the time frame, allowing for an accurate representation of malaria incidence by capturing cases as they occur. In contrast, passive case detection uses existing healthcare systems to identify incoming patients and their contact networks, which, although practical, is known to underestimate the true incidence of malaria due to reliance on symptomatic individuals seeking treatment \citet{rios-zertuche_performance_2021}.
    \item[Parasite Prevalence]
    Representing the proportion of the population infected with malaria at any given time, parasite prevalence is determined through cross-sectional surveys. This measure includes asymptomatic infections, offering a broader perspective on the infection's spread. However, the prevalence is influenced by the diagnostic methods used, with  RDTs and microscopy detecting fewer low density infections compared to PCR testing. 
    \item[Entomological Inoculation Rate]
    EIR estimates the average number of infectious mosquito bites received by a person per year. This rate is subject to considerable variability, influenced by the methods used for mosquito capture and the estimation of the sporozoite rate, as not all mosquito bites result in transmission. The EIR's variability is further compounded by environmental heterogeneity and seasonal factors, such as rainfall and temperature, which affect mosquito breeding and survival rates.
\end{description}
\end{thesisquote}

All of these metrics are heavily influenced by the vectorial capacity, the rate of contact between infectious mosquitoes and susceptible humans. However, a myriad of confounding factors, such as demographics, seasonality, human habitats, land use, diagnostic methods and wider malaria controls can obscure their precise relationship. Understanding these relationships are essential for predicting and mitigating malaria burden, and so have been the subject of considerable research \citet{griffin_estimates_2014,griffin_is_2016,cameron_defining_2015,reiker_emulator-based_2021}. Of these efforts, we use the transmission model proposed by \citet{griffin_estimates_2014} as a baseline. Which we describe below:

\paragraph{The baseline transmission model.} In \citet{griffin_estimates_2014}, human transmission is disaggregated into age and heterogeneity compartments. The average age and heterogeneity of biting rates. The force of infection for each compartment is influenced by the age group - with adults experiencing more bites - and heterogeneity group. Immunity levels is accrued on each exposure to malaria. Immunity types are divided into pre-erythrocytic, acquired clinical immunity, maternal clinical immunity and immunity to detection by microscopy. The probability of clinical, asymptomatic and subpatent infection for each compartment is derived from latent force of infection and immunity levels. Taken at equilibrium, this transmission offers a closed-form solution for incidence and prevalence across age compartments for a given Entomological Inoculation Rate (EIR). The full model is presented in the Appendix \ref{section:equilibrium}

\subsection{Agentic model discovery}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have increasingly been applied to scientific model discovery. Since models can be expressed in code, and LLMs excel in code generation \citep{Jimenez2023Oct}, it follows that LLMs should be able to generate scientific models. In the field of Symbolic Regression, where the goal is to find expressions which regress to data, \citep{Shojaee2024Apr} have shown that a program search can propose accurate expressions in Biology and Physics problems more efficiently than the previous state of the art - evolutionary search, even on tasks which do not appear in the LLMs training data.

Since, there have been extensions which apply program search to discover probabilistic models. This is more challenging than symbolic regression, as evaluation is more computationally expensive and distributional metrics are more complex to reason about simple residuals. BoxLM \citep{Li2024Feb} proposed a process where an LLM synthesises a program and subsequently critiques it according to the Bayesian Workflow \citep{Gelman2020Nov}. G-Sim \citep{Holt2025Jun} and ModelSMC \cite{Wahl2026Feb} instead incorporated the SBI framework to allow models without tractable likelihoods, with ModelSMC in particular casting search as principled sample from the space of models given observation data.

There has been limited research on search parameterisation and the choice of algorithm, leaving it to trail and error. The most popular algorithm is the ``Islands model'', where proposed models are divided into islands for resampling in order to maintain diversity, which is adopted by FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, LLM-SR, ShinkaEvolve \citep{Romera-Paredes2024Jan,Novikov2025Jun,Shojaee2024Apr,Lange2025Sep}. Despite these advances in search algorithms, some would argue that Independant Identically Distributed (iid) samples from the LLM remain competitive \citep{Gideoni2026Feb}. The algorithms for program search are sensitive to the task and search parameters, with frameworks leaving many options to the user including: model size, number of agents, prompt, diagnostics, starting model, etc. We focus on iid sampling in this work as it remains the simplest search algorithm and is less likely to confound our investigations into the influence of search parameterisations.

No LLM driven program search has been applied to malaria transmission modelling at the time of writing. It remains to be seen if LLMs can propose competitive models in a reasonable compute budget and if so, what the proposed models can tell us about the landscape of highly predictive, mechanistic models.

\subsection{Contributions}

\paragraph{We establish a baseline for a malaria transmission model.} We recreate the malaria modelling study published by \citet{griffin_estimates_2014}. We identify and remediate some shortcomings in the methodology, and define evaluation criteria for future modelling efforts. The code for the model is published at \citep{Charles2024}, and the fitting and diagnostics are published at \citep{Charles2026Msinf}

\paragraph{We compare various search parameterisations for iid LLM proposals.} We investigate the effects of LLM model size, starting model, modelling scope, and incorporating tool calling into model proposition. The resulting model proposals are evaluated against common Bayesian modelling and MCMC sampling statistics to estimate the influence of different search design decisions. The harness for iid search is published at \citep{Charles2026Disc}.

\paragraph{We provide an analysis of motifs which influence malaria transmission model performance.} We use the proposed models from the previous contribution to infer structural insights into malaria transmission modelling choices which could lead to more predictive and identifiable models.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Model fitting and evaluation}

We used numpyro \citep{numpyro} for fitting, as it provides a wide array of MCMC kernels and integrates tightly with JAX's GPU acceleration and auto-differentiation functionality. As a gradient-free kernel, we used the Sample Adaptive (SA) kernel \citep{Zhu2019}. SA was parameterised with 1,000/1,000 warmup/posterior samples. As a gradient based kernel we used the No-U-Turn-Sampler (NUTS) \citep{nuts}. NUTS was parameterised with 100/100 warmup/posterior samples.

%Pathfinder
We explored three intiaialisation methods on the baseline and then used the best and most general for model search. Median initialisation sets the first samples to the prior median value. Griffin initialisation sets the first samples to the median estimate published in \citet{griffin}, since EIR estimates were unpublished they are set to the prior median value. Pathfinder initialisation \citep{Zhang2022}, estimates well behaved initial samples by applying a variational inference optimisation for a Normal approximation. We parameterised Pathfinder to independently optimise initialisations for each chain.

MCMC traces were then evaluated on a range of common metrics for prediction quality and convergence. To assess model likelihood for model selection, we computed the Expected Log Probability Density using Leave One Study Out (ELPD-LOSO) on a training and held out set. The observations are partitioned by randomly selecting three studies to hold out. The ``training'' set was made available to agents to analyse before making their proposals, while the held out set was kept hidden. To assess the identifiability of a parameter, we compute the KL divergence of the posterior estimate from the prior. A low divergence, or little update, suggests that the parameter is unidentifiable. Beyond model likelihood and identifiability, we also evaluated posterior coverage, $\hat{r}$, Effective Sample Size (ESS), and time to fit in minutes.

\subsection{Model search}

To orchestrate the model search, we built Discovery. Discovery collects the search context, including a brief, the starting point and search objective into a prompt for the agent. It also, constructs an isolated temporary directory with observational and supporting data for the agent to analyse through a tool calling interface. To interface with the underlying model, Discovery supports either an OpenAI compatible URL, or execution of a headless coding harness (Claude Code or Codex) in a subprocess. For the open weight experiments, we launched a vLLM server with Qwen 3.6 112B (is that right?) \citep{qwen}. For the frontier experiments we launched Claude Code and Codex with Pro subscriptions. After the agents have made their proposals, Discovery extracts, filters and evaluates them. To save computation, we filter proposals by (...). The remaining proposals are then fit using NUTS with Pathfinder initialisation and evaluated using the same metrics outlined above.

\section{Local model search}

After establishing our baseline and evaluation metrics, we conducted various searches to see alternative models compare.

\paragraph{Starting point}

One non-obvious search parameter is whether to start the search from \emph{scratch} or from the \emph{incumbent} model. To start from \emph{scratch} practically means to present a deliberately low performing, trivial model to demonstrate the function signature to the model. The benefit of this approach is that there is less steering and the search is more likely to explore new ground. To start from the \emph{incumbent} model is to provide it to the model proposer at the start of the search, akin to a model correction set up. The benefit of this approach is that the model can start from a more complex point, benefitting from prior research into the modelling problem and produce models which are more interpretable to those familiar with the existing model.

\paragraph{Search aperture}

An orthogonal parameter is the search aperture, specifying the editable parts of the model code, and thereby restricting the scope of the model search. We proposed two model search apertures for our baseline in particular: \emph{curves} and \emph{immunity}.

The \emph{Curves} aperture specifies the wider aperture where the search spans sub-models which are conditioned on EIR, treatment fractions, and demographics to generate prevalence and incidence for each age group - equilibrium prevalence/incidence vs age is the curve here, not the traditional prevalence/incidence vs time trajectories in dynamical models. The study effects and the observational model remain out of scope, this is to make model propositions comparable and to mitigate search hacking - where the proposer edits the model likelihood without changes to the mechanism to produce a more likely model (examples have been cited in \citep{Karwowski2026Aug}).

The \emph{Immunity} aperture specifies a narrower search where sub-models are conditioned on Force of Infection (by age) and generate probabilities for pre-erythrocytic infection, clinical infection and detection by microscopy. This leaves the incumbent's formulation of heterogeneity, and equilibrium prevalence and incidence from infection probabilities untouched.

We performed a sweep to search over all combinations of starting point and search aperture (n=4). Each search consisted of 8 agents drawn from Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8 at temperature 1.0, with max\_tokens=32768, thinking\_tokens=2048 and a budget of 50 tool calls per agent, resulting in 32 + 4 models - 4 incumbents and 32 proposals. Of the 32 proposals, 13 compiled and fit.

\section{Frontier model search}

To investigate the gap between local models and the frontier, we also ran searches with Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's ChatGPT, through the claude code and codex harnesses respectively. We searched from the incumbent starting point with the curves aperture, as it was the most promising configuration for mechanistic model search in the local search experiment. We tried two different model sizes for each model provider: Haiku 4.5 (\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}) and Opus 5 (\texttt{claude-opus-5}) from Anthropic, and GPT-5.6 luna (\texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}) and GPT-5.6 sol (\texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}) from OpenAI. A major difference from the previous experiment is that we imposed a 15 minute time limit to restrict the costs for a search and make it acheivable with a base subscription, future work may wish to explore API billing to make this run comparable with the previous experiment. Each search consisted of 8 agents per model, giving 32 proposals which were fitted alongside the incumbent under the same pathfinder initialisation and the same three-study holdout as the local searches. Of the 32 proposals, 24 compiled and fit: 8 of 8 for Haiku, 4 of 8 for Opus, 4 of 8 for luna and 8 of 8 for sol. The results for this search are shown alongside the local proposals in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}.

\section{Model landscape}

Across all proposals used Claude Opus 5 to find structural motifs to help describe the qualitative approaches taken by the agents. The most common approach (M1) was to consolidate the three immunity functions (pre-erythrocytic, clinical and detection immunity), which generally improved ESS, fit time and identifiability compared to approaches which did not, see Table \ref{tab:motif_sensitivity} for a sensitivity analysis of the motifs. However, the proposal spaces were very diverse, with all but one search (scr/imm) producing proposals with $\geq 5$ unique motif sets (out of 8 proposals). A detailed breakdown of motif attribution is shown in Table \ref{tab:motifs}. We describe each motif below:

\paragraph{M1. Immunity consolidation:} collapse IB/IC/ID into one or two shared immunity levels, or share their decay and shape constants.

\paragraph{M2. Reduced exposure dependency on clinical infection:} Add dampening factors to the exposure that builds IC. Or compute $\phi$ from age instead of exposure.

\paragraph{M3. Simplified detectability:} simplify or remove the age-dependent detectability modifier $f_d$ (fd0, ad0, gd).

\paragraph{M4. Pure Hill saturation:} remove the asymptote floors $b_1$ and $\phi_1$.

\paragraph{M5. Constrained Hill shape:} fix or tie the Hill shape exponents ($k=1$, $k$ shared, or hard-coded).

\paragraph{M6. Immunity acts on infection duration:} the acquired level raises the clearance rate for asymptomatic infection $r_A$, not only detectability. For example,

(incumbent):

$$r_A = \mathrm{constant}$$

(opus 11):

$$r_A(a) = r_A^{0}\,\big(1 + r_{a1}(1 - h_d)\big), \qquad h_d = \frac{1}{1+(I_A/I_{D0})^{k_d}}$$

\paragraph{M7. Saturating force of infection in EIR:} Impose a cap on force of infection relative to EIR. For example,

(incumbent):

$$\lambda = b\varepsilon$$

(luna 4):

$$\lambda = \frac{b\varepsilon}{1 + b\varepsilon/\lambda_{\max}}, \qquad \lambda_{\max} = 0.25\ \text{d}^{-1}$$

\paragraph{M8. Maternal immunity simplified:} Either remove maternal immunity completely, or simplify its computation from a bin averaged integral to a pointwise exponential.

\paragraph{M9. Algebraic surface:} completely replace the compartmental equilibrium for an expression relating age to EIR. For example, with age $a$ in years and annual EIR $\varepsilon$,

(sol 12):

$$\mathrm{prev}_m(a) = p_{\max}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon + \varepsilon_p}\,\Big(1 - e^{-(a/a_p)^{1.35}}\Big)\Big(\delta + \frac{1-\delta}{1+(a/11)^{1.6}}\Big)\,(1 - 0.55 f_T)$$

$$\mathrm{inc}(a) = 10\,\kappa\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon + \varepsilon_c}\,\big(1 - e^{-a/0.65}\big)\,e^{-a/\tau(\varepsilon)}, \qquad \tau(\varepsilon) = \frac{\tau_0}{1 + \sqrt{\varepsilon/\varepsilon_c}}$$

for free parameters $\tau_0, \kappa, \varepsilon_c, p_{\max}, \varepsilon_p, a_p, \delta$.

%   (identical to the source fitted in 17-codex-luna-sol-16-pathfinder, index 12).
%   Symbols: p_max=prev_max, eps_p=prev_e50, a_p=prev_rise, delta=prev_tail,
%   kappa=inc_max_frac, eps_c=inc_e50, tau_0=inc_tau; all other constants are hard-coded.

\paragraph{M10. Rewrite age dependent biting:} change the biting and exposure age profile ($\psi$).

\paragraph{M11. Non-constant immunity decay:} change immunity decay based on exposure or a combination of decay lengths.

\section{Discussion}

We have presented an evaluation harness and baseline for malaria transmission model search. This step allows researchers to quantitatively explore the modelling space with respect to model likelihood (through ELPD) and computational requirements for fitting (through MCMC statistics).

The current search results suggest that there is potential to simplify the incumbent model and improve fitting, without impacting predictive performance. It remains to be seen if preditive accuracy can be improved.

There is a big performance gap between the open weight models (and custom harness) used in this study and the frontier models (with frontier coding harnesses). Of the open weight model proposals, less than half compiled and fit (compared to $\frac{3}{4}$ frontier proposals).

These early results also suggest that human judgement is still required to discern which models to deploy, many proposals make structural changes to the model which result in no significant change in predictive performance. In the absence of more data to discern true mechanisms, humans must validate that the proposed mechanisms are at least consistent with our best understanding of malaria transmission.

\subsection{Future work}

We should investigate providing MCMC traces to the agents. This data was witheld and agents were steered away from running MCMC fits to make the best use of limited compute/tokens and to avoid reward hacking behaviours. However, the trace for the incumbent fit could provide more direct signals on how to remediate misspesification. 

LLM-based methods are sensitive to prompt optimisation, which has been unexplored in this study. The prompt is long, as it provides context about the nuanced malaria model fitting problem, the resources available for analysis, the evaluation process and praticalities about proposal submission. It assumes little knowledge of domain specific techniques or issues the agents may consider or encounter (outside of the incumbent model code), and does not prescribe probabilistic modelling workflows to consider (outside of numpyro). For malaria modelling in particular, there is potential to incorporate the insights from this paper into the prompt to accelerate the search. However, in the interest of a more general model search method, we would recommend optimising prompts by reducing the size and increasing its clarity.

The effect of the agent harness and underlying model has not yet been disentangled. Discovery, the custom harness developed for the open weight models, is a rudimentary prototype and does not compare with Claude Code or Codex in terms of functionality. One should investigate a fully featured harness which supports any model, such as OpenCode \citep{OpenCode}, to put them on a level playing field and remove any barriers introduced by a poorly defined interface.

A careful scale up of resources alongside search improvements could lead to progress on the predictive performance. The open weight model (Qwen 3.6) was run on a single HPC node with four L40s. There have since been many releases of more performant and larger models which could be executed with a multi-node set up. The frontier models were restricted to 15 minutes, and inference time scaling is known to greatly improve response quality and has been extended to the order of days. However, most of the proposals in this study exhibited worse predictive performance than the incumbent, and therefore care is needed to avoid wasting computationally expensive MCMC fits on unlikely trajectories.

The underlying data used in this study is limited and should be extended. For consistency with previous fits, we omitted observations in subjects which were older than 20 years old and assumed an exponential mortality rate. Observations in older subjects would confirm or rule out mechanisms through emergent behaviours which only appear on longer time scales. More realistic demographics can be simulated from WorldPop data, and is currently supported by dmeq. Much more paired prevalence and incidence data across age groups has since been collected and standardised. With the help of agents, one can extend the evaluation data set with new points which reasonably control for interventions, seasonality and other covariates in order to reduce the variance of ELPD.
